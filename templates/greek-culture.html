Greek Culture

History

Classical Antiquity covers the Mediterranean basin from the 8th century BCE to the 5th century CE, with literature studies often focusing up to the 3rd century CE before the spread of Christianity reshaped the cultural landscape.

Greek Society

  • Geography: mountainous land, many valleys (protection), every polis near the sea. Located at the crossroads between Egypt and Mesopotamia;
  • Colonialism: a polis equipped a ship, and young settlers founded a new community that kept ties with the mother city;
  • Greeks felt a shared identity as Hellenes.

Features of Greek Culture

  • Harmony is beauty:
    • Cosmologism: the cosmos is ordered, so beauty = measure = organisation. Excess is ugly, ostracism checked anyone rising too high (gods grow envious);
    • Ideal person is beautiful in body and soul (kalokagathia). Physical perfection culminates in the Olympic Games; spiritual development means the arts, science and philosophy;
    • Core aesthetic categories are beauty, measure, harmony. Polykleitos' Canon worked out the proportions of the human body (e.g. head = 1/7 of height, face = 1/10), it's reflected in sculpture.
  • Anthropocentrism: "man is the measure of all things"; gods look human; life is preferred to death;
  • Rationalism:
    • philosophy is separate from myth and religion;
    • Hesiod's Theogony systematises myth;
    • science is independent (geometry, medicine, applied mathematics in crafts and trade);
    • Pythagoreans believed that the universe and man's place in it are mathematical proportions. They organized the cosmos around ten fundamental pairs of opposites, such as limit and unlimited, odd and even, and light and darkness.
  • Agon (competition): citizens constantly competed in politics, law, theatre, crafts, sport (Olympic games) and education. Prizes: laurel wreaths, lifelong maintenance by the polis;
  • Other traits: optimism, tolerance, respect for law, symmetry, a cyclical sense of time (no idea of progress), and low individuality (Classical statues).

Arts

    Temples
  • The main building in the city, and cities competed in building temples;
  • A peripteros is surrounded by columns (Parthenon);
  • No difference between inside and outside, the architects rejected the interior.

Pottery shapes: amphora (storage), hydria (water), kylix/skyphos (drinking cup), krater (mixing wine and water), oinochoe (pouring wine), lekythos/alabastron (oil, perfume), pelike, stamnos (storage).

Periodization of Greek Literature

  • Pre-literary: myth, folklore;
    • Ancient Europe, 3rd millennium BC;
    • Aegean culture. Connection to nature. Significant role of women, cult of the Great Goddess. Chthonic beings (earth);
    • Minoan civilization, c.2000 – c.1450 BC;
    • Mycenaean civilization, c.1700 – c.1100 BC. Indo-European influence. Male dominance. Principal deities associated with the sky. Struggle against the Aegean legacy and a complete reinterpretation of the old culture's deities. Destruction of cities and loss of writing as consequences of the 12th-century BC catastrophe (the "Dark Ages");
    • Olympian mythology forms, c. 1200 BC.
  • Archaic: Homer, Hesiod, lyric;
  • Classical;
  • Hellenistic;
  • Roman.

Pre-literary

Features of Greek mythology:

  • myths were recorded late;
  • several versions of one myth (e.g. Medea);
  • layering of different mythologies, influence of Indo-European tribes;
  • anthropomorphism;
  • importance of Fate: the Moirai stand above the gods.

Gods: Zeus (supreme god, thunder), his companions Moirai, Horai, Charites, Poseidon, Hades, Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hermes, Aphrodite, Ares, Athena, Hephaestus, Artemis, Apollo, Dionysus.

Folklore Genres

  • songs: for work; drinking (skolia); ritual for funeral, wedding, calendar (paean, dithyramb, partheneion);
  • proverbs;
  • fables, tales (a tale has a moral; myth is "truth"), heroic legends.
Performers of folklore: aoidos (improvising composer-performer); rhapsode (performer-collector).

Archaic

Black-figure pottery became common between the 7th and 5th centuries BC. Red-figure pottery developed in Athens around 520 BC, replaced black-figure pottery and remained in use until the late 3rd century AD.

Sculpture

Kouros (nude youth) and Kore (draped girl) are stiff statues, with an "archaic smile," used as grave markers or thank-offerings.

Architecture

Orders of temples

  • Doric Order. The oldest and simplest order, appearing around the 7th century BCE on the Greek mainland. Heavy, stocky columns with plain, rounded capitals and no base (Parthenon);
  • Ionic Order. Originated in Ionia (modern-day Turkey) around the mid-6th century BCE. It is taller and more slender, recognizable by scroll-like capitals called volutes and a distinct column base.

Classical

Sculpture

Fluid movement in sculpture appears (Discobolus by Myron, 460-450 BCE, the original Greek bronze cast is lost, the work is known through Roman copies. Doryphoros by Polykleitos, around 440 BCE, the bronze original is lost).

4. Epic: Homer Epic puts the collective above the individual. The epic hero stands out but remains fused with his clan (Losev). Homeric question: Wolf's analytical theory; Lachmann's "small songs"; Hermann's "kernel" theory; the Unitarians (one author, symmetric composition); Butler (the Odyssey written by a woman). Dating: 9th to 8th c. BC, text fixed in the 6th c. BC, split into 24 books in the 3rd c. BC. The Troy of the 13th c. BC was excavated by Schliemann (1870s). The oldest complete Iliad manuscript is Venetus A (10th c.). Other poems of the Trojan Cycle (Cypria) survive only in fragments. Social background (Losev): clan aristocracy with luxury, feasts and a growing sense of private property; developed crafts; early trade (disguised as gift exchange or plunder); patriarchal slavery with privileged slaves (Eumaeus, Eurycleia) and harsh punishment (the maids); governance by kings, council of elders and assembly. Features of the epic: Traditional, calm, monumental, objective; constant forward action; aristocratic bias; physical depiction of the world, even of psychology ("it is beautiful to fight, but also to sleep and eat"). Ethics = aesthetics: loyalty to the clan, honour and glory, pride, courage, respect for tradition and elders, xenia (hospitality). Style: fixed and compound epithets ("helmet-flashing Hector," "swift-footed Achilles"), long similes from nature and farming, repetition (up to a third of the text, from oral origins), slowness, detail, retardation (digressions, e.g. the nurse washing Odysseus' feet), chronological incompatibility, hexameter, Ionic dialect with Aeolic elements. The Iliad covers about 50 days and the Odyssey about 40 (synecdoche: one episode reveals a whole phenomenon). Heroes by antithesis: Achilles (tribal, unbridled) vs. Hector (city hero, family man); Patroclus (timid youth) vs. Diomedes (warlike); Nestor (wise old age) vs. Priam (tragic old age); Penelope (faithful) vs. Clytemnestra (unfaithful); Calypso vs. Nausicaa; Helen is seen only through others' eyes. Odysseus is the most many-sided: strong, clever, cruel, faithful, trickster-like. Gods: not moral ideals. They take sides (Apollo and Aphrodite for Troy; Hera and Athena for the Achaeans), influence actions and feelings, but can be read as personifications of inner decisions. Heroes sometimes talk back to them; Fate is powerful. 5. Didactic Epic: Hesiod, Works and Days Didactic epic gives moral or practical instruction. It arises after private property appears and heroic values are reconsidered. First work written for a personal occasion: advice to his brother Perses. Themes: labour and justice. Zeus is the guardian of justice and judge of wrongdoers. Part 1 is ethical (criticism of "gift-devouring kings," myths of the Ages of Man and Pandora). Part 2 is practical farming advice with summer and winter descriptions. Style: oral features, strings of advice, repetition, formulas, ring composition, simple syntax, few vivid images, leitmotifs, symmetry. Hexameter is used for a content opposite to Homer's. Legacy: Alexandrian "learned" poetry and Virgil's Georgics. 6. Lyric Poetry (7th to 5th c. BC) Causes: stronger poleis, end of clan order, tyrants, colonisation, rising individual and private initiative. Lyric = poetry sung or recited to lyre or flute; short, expresses personal feeling, based on metre (long/short syllables), no rhyme. Recitative lyric Elegy (couplet of hexameter + pentameter): Callinus (military-patriotic), Tyrtaeus (glory, beautiful death in youth), Mimnermus (spring, youth, first love theme), Solon (philosophical; statesman; measure and harmony), Theognis (embittered aristocrat; hates democracy; state as a ship run by drunken sailors). Iambus (satirical, folk): Archilochus (son of an aristocrat and a slave; mercenary; first voice of the individual; rejects Homeric values; influenced Catullus and Horace), Hipponax (choliambus, "lame iambus"; parody; beggar-jester), Semonides of Amorgos (didactic; satire on women compared to animals). Melic (sung) lyric Solo (Aegean islands): Alcaeus (Lesbos; aristocrat in politics; allegory of the state as a ship; wine and male friendship), Sappho (Lesbos; led a girls' circle devoted to Aphrodite; love as an exhausting, all-consuming force, "love-pain, love-death"; Plato called her the tenth Muse), Anacreon (light, mocking; love as a festival; wine; influenced Pushkin, Zhukovsky, Voltaire). Choral (mainly Sparta; hymns, dithyrambs): Alcman (first choral poet; wrote words, music and dances), Ibycus, Simonides of Ceos (epinikia, victory odes; funeral poems), Pindar (commissioned odes; kalokagathia, measure, reason; first mentions transmigration, hell and paradise; shaped the European ode), Bacchylides (nephew of Simonides; elegant, clear, plastic). 7. Theatre Origin: tragedy ("goat song") grew from the dithyramb sung by satyr choruses for Dionysus. Peisistratus founded the Great Dionysia; Thespis added an actor who answered the chorus. Features: a major role for the chorus; myth-based plots (exceptions: Aeschylus' Persians, Phrynichus' Capture of Miletus); aesthetic and didactic purpose. Festival: three days; each poet presented a tetralogy (three tragedies + a satyr play). The archon assigned a choregos (a wealthy citizen who paid for and trained the chorus). Judges were chosen by lot; the protagonist actor was honoured. Performed in the open air in a theatron, with an orchestra, masks, long robes and high kothornoi; men played all roles. Tragedy structure: prologue, parodos (chorus entry), stasimon (chorus song), episode (dialogue), kommos (shared lament), exodos. Aeschylus (525 to 456 BC) Fought in the Persian Wars; democratic, optimistic, values compromise and measure. Gods reward good and punish evil (early gods are harsher). Hereditary guilt coexists with personal responsibility. Innovations: second actor, scenery, special effects; static dramatic devices such as long silences. Prometheus Bound: old vs. new gods; Prometheus the philanthropist vs. tyrant Zeus; fire = enlightenment; conscious self-sacrifice (contrast with Io); oratorical, little action. Oresteia: Agamemnon (Clytemnestra's crime), Libation Bearers (Orestes' revenge), Eumenides (the Furies vs. new gods). The curse on Atreus' line is resolved by humane institutions (the court). Sophocles (496 to 406 BC) Lived in Pericles' Golden Age and shows society at its peak. Gods are all-powerful but rarely on stage. Heroes are individual, act independently and are "as they should be." Chorus becomes lyrical commentary. Innovations: third actor, stichomythia. Oedipus the King: a tragedy of fate stressing the fickleness of fortune and the limits of human knowledge; Oedipus actively takes moral responsibility, punishing himself; no villains (error, not intent); an analytic drama unfolding from the past. Antigone: conflict between eternal (divine) law (Antigone) and state law (Creon); justice triumphs without gods intervening; contrast of Antigone and timid Ismene. Euripides (c. 480 to 406 BC) Lived through the Peloponnesian War and the crisis of democracy; faith in gods and justice declines, the goddess Tyche (chance) rises. Suffering is irrational, not divine justice. Innovations: philosophical debates, psychology (even pathology: Bacchae, Heracles), demythologising (Andromache, Electra, Orestes), intrigue tragedies (Helen, Iphigenia in Tauris, Ion; they anticipate late-antique fiction), private life, sophistic "each side has its truth," heroes torn between feeling and norms, deus ex machina endings, kings as beggars in rags, slaves portrayed as morally equal or better, critique of marriage, sympathetic women (Alcestis as true partner), scepticism toward oracles, monodies and agons, colloquial language, shrinking chorus. Medea: conflict between one passion and another; ordinary people. Hippolytus: gods are passionate and cruel (Aphrodite vengeful; Artemis arrives too late: gods don't oppose each other). 8. Comedy and Aristophanes Origin: Aristotle traces it to phallic songs (fertility processions with mocking skits); komos = revelling band; chorus costumes (Wasps, Birds, Frogs). Features: stock masks (braggart soldier, charlatan); topical, political and satirical; real people named (Cleon, Socrates, Euripides); fantastic plots in which the hero wins a contest and establishes a new, upside-down order of abundance, ending in a wedding or komos procession. Structure: prologue (fantastic plan), parodos, agon (debate between two sides), parabasis (chorus addresses the audience for the poet), farcical scenes, komos. Aristophanes: works from the Peloponnesian War, moderately democratic, voicing war-weary Attic farmers; a valuable historical source. Knights: against Cleon. Clouds: mocks sophistry via Socrates as a caricature; Just vs. Unjust Argument, and the Unjust wins. Frogs: literary contest of Aeschylus and Euripides (earliest literary criticism); criticises Euripides for lacking a heroic ideal. Lysistrata: anti-war. From Birds on, satire weakens and the chorus shrinks, with a turn to social utopia. ...
