Homeric question

  • Unitarian theory: the epics possess such a strong, deliberate artistic, thematic, and dramatic unity that they must be the work of a single, master poet, traditionally named Homer;
  • Butler: Homer only authored the Iliad. Odyssey is uniquely woman-centered compared to other ancient epics, featuring prominent, independent female figures (Athena, Circe, Calypso, and Penelope) and less flattering or weaker portrayals of male characters.
  • Hermann's "kernel" theory: there was a pristine, shorter original kernel (an "Ur-Iliad") that was gradually expanded, altered, and built upon by generations of later, less-talented poets;
  • Lachmann's "small songs": Iliad was a compilation of 18 separate, autonomous folk songs or short lay poems that were originally composed by different bards and later stitched together into a single text;
  • Wolf's analytical theory: the epics were the composite products of multiple oral poets, stitched together centuries later by subsequent editors.

Features of the epics

Features:

  • Traditional themes (war, love);
  • Calmness: the author above the fray;
  • Monumentality: depiction of great things;
  • Objectivity, tangible portrayal of the world, including the inner life;
  • Ethical ideals:
    • loyalty to the clan (the hero remembers even distant ancestors and places collective, or ancestral values above personal ones. Acting wrongly brings disgrace upon all ancestors and descendants);
    • respect for traditions (e.g., Glaucus exchanging armor with Diomedes) and for elders (attitude toward Nestor);
    • glory, pride, courage;
    • xenia, the law of hospitality.
  • Active agency. Even when the hero is sleeping, standing, or talking, he drives the action forward. It is always a narrative;
  • Predominantly aristocratic outlook, yet Odysseus carves a chair and Nausicaa washes clothes herself; there is no significant distinction. Slaves are referred to as 'noble'.

Epic heroes

  • Perform feats beyond the capability of an ordinary person, portrayed in hyperbolic terms;
  • Have individuality, each hero possesses distinct traits (Odysseus is cunning, Achilles prioritizes his personal honor and grievance, Hector fights for his family);
  • Complex/ambiguous figures;
  • Presented through antithesis:
    • Achilles vs Hector.
      Achilles is the quintessential epic hero. He is utterly unbridled, as seen in the dialogue between Achilles and Hector before the final battle. A hero of the tribe.
      Hector is a hero of the city, more civilized. Is the only one shown in the context of family life). Both know their fate, but Hector also knows that Troy will fall after his death;
    • Patroclus (timid youth) vs Diomedes (the most warlike) — the youngest;
    • Nestor (the wisest, representing a glorious old age) vs Priam (represents a tragic old age) — the oldest;
    • Agamemnon vs Menelaus vs Paris. Agamemnon is decisive and arrogant, yet acknowledges his mistakes. Menelaus is less decisive. Paris is a strong warrior but prefers leisure and love;
    • Ajax son of Telamon (a heavy warrior) vs Ajax son of Oileus (a light warrior who attacks and moves swiftly);
    • Penelope (faithful wife) vs Clytemnestra (unfaithful wife);
    • Calypso vs Nausicaa. Calypso is a nymph living alone on an island. Nausicaa is a young maiden surrounded by her companions;
    • Helen is portrayed solely through the eyes of other characters;
    • Odysseus is the most multifaceted hero: strong, intelligent, and ruthless (he slaughters the suitors), yet a faithful husband and a desirable lover. He frequently changes his appearance—rendering him unrecognizable—and bears a resemblance to a trickster figure (he even descends into the Underworld).

Gods

  • Do not embody justice, moral ideals, or morality;
  • They take an active part in human life and take sides (Apollo and Aphrodite support Troy; Hera and Athena support the Achaeans).
    • Humans are playthings of the gods. They influence human lives (Poseidon prevents Odysseus from returning home) and prompt specific actions (the Trojan Pandarus fires into the Greek camp—treacherously violating a newly concluded truce—because he is influenced by Pallas Athena) as well as feelings and emotions (when Priam enters Achilles' tent and a friendly rapport develops between them, it is because the gods have instilled this in them);
    • If the plot is interpreted literally, humans appear debased in Homer’s works—mere soulless instruments of the gods—with the gods themselves serving as the true heroes of the epic;
    • However, the Homeric gods can also be viewed as a personification of human feelings and moods. When a human action is attributed to the command of a deity, it effectively means the action stems from the individual's own internal decision;
  • Significant role of fate;
  • Heroes (Agamemnon, Achilles, Menelaus) defy the gods, sometimes quite rudely;
  • The poems occupy a transitional position between ancient fatalism and the human freedom characteristic of later eras.

Style

  • Chronological inconsistency — simultaneous events presented as sequential;
  • Leisurely narrative pace, reflecting the idea that great matters require unhurried telling;
  • Repetition accounting for up to a third of the text, stemming from the tradition of oral storytelling;
  • Retardation — slowing the narrative through lyrical digressions and descriptive passages (the scene where a maid washes Odysseus’s feet upon his return);
  • Compound epithets (прекраснообутый, прекраснокудрявая, прекраснозданный, прекрасногривые, прекрасновенчанная, крепкосозданный, шлемоблещущий;);
  • Fixed epithets (шлемоблещущий Гектор, благодорный Эврисфей, быстроногий Ахиллес);
  • Similes drawn from nature and agricultural labor — Homer lived during a time of peace;
  • Attention to detail (the death of Pandarus);
  • Composed in hexameter.
